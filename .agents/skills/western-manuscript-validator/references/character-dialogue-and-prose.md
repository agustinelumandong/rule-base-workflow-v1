# Character, Dialogue, and Prose Validation

Western character is proved through choices under practical pressure. Voice grows from person, place, work, education, class, age, relationship, and immediate objective—not from a single stock rhythm.

## Primary Layer Guidance

Use **Prose** for unclear wording, duplicated interpretation, exposition disguised as speech, or cast-wide voice sameness. Use **Tone** for era-, region-, subgenre-, or character-register mismatch. Use **Story** when behavior, motive, knowledge, or physical action itself does not work. Use **Continuity** when the passage violates supplied prior facts. Use **Pacing** when an exchange has no meaningful turn and exists mainly as recap. Choose one primary layer.

## Useful Principles Without Absolutes

Common strengths include:

- subtext;
- character-specific voice;
- period and regional plausibility;
- purposeful dialogue;
- restrained dialect spelling;
- supporting characters with their own wants;
- concrete behavior instead of generic archetype labels;
- silence or action beats that carry meaning.

Economy, silence, and indirection are common strengths, not universal laws. A preacher, gambler, rancher, teacher, child, soldier, widow, and drover should not all sound terse in the same way.

## Character Through Behavior

For every major character in a scene or chapter, ask:

- What is the **current want**?
- What distinctive action does this character take that another cast member would not?
- What **habit**, **fear**, **skill**, **debt**, **wound**, flaw, promise, class position, or obligation shapes the choice?
- What does the character refuse to say or do?
- What practical competence solves one kind of problem but fails against another?
- Does the action change a relationship, situation, responsibility, or available choice?
- Does the character repeat an old strategy before changing it?
- Is the final change practical and visible, or only named in narration?

### Individualization tests

Look for specific:

- tools and how they are maintained;
- work habits;
- scars, injuries, and compensations;
- debts and promises;
- grudges and loyalties;
- rituals, mistakes, and superstitions;
- education and reading ability;
- relationship to animals, money, law, violence, family, faith, and authority;
- tasks the character avoids because their main competence cannot solve them.

A gunfighter with guilt is still generic unless the guilt changes behavior, relationships, and choices in a specific way.

## Thought-Over-Behavior

Potential signal words include:

- knew;
- realized;
- understood;
- felt;
- wanted;
- decided;
- it meant;
- as if.

These words are not forbidden. Flag them when they:

1. repeat what physical behavior already proves;
2. state meaning immediately before or after the scene demonstrates it;
3. replace a choice, silence, mistake, delay, tool use, or consequence that should carry the emotion;
4. explain another character's intention without viewpoint access;
5. convert ambiguity into author commentary.

### Deletion test

Delete the sentence mentally. If clarity and emotional force survive, cut it. If necessary information disappears, decide whether interiority is the best form or whether behavior should establish it.

Interiority remains valid when it supplies memory, contradiction, private judgment, false belief, or a decision the body cannot fully show.

## Spoon-Feeding

Flag:

- narration that announces an action's purpose before the action;
- narration that interprets an image the reader already understands;
- repeated explanation after dialogue;
- tactical inventories that tell the purpose of every move;
- thematic summaries placed in a character's mouth;
- statements that remove intended uncertainty.

Preserve necessary orientation. Do not confuse clarity with spoon-feeding.

## Dialogue: Character Ownership

For each speaker, test:

- age;
- education and literacy;
- occupation;
- region and migration history;
- class and status;
- temperament;
- relationship to the listener;
- immediate objective;
- what the speaker risks by being direct;
- habitual sentence length, vocabulary, humor, evasion, formality, and profanity.

A line fails ownership when any character could say it without changing wording or effect.

## Dialogue Purpose and Friction

An exchange should usually do at least one important thing and may do several:

- pursue a goal;
- resist another goal;
- reveal unequal knowledge;
- conceal or distort information;
- alter trust;
- negotiate work, danger, debt, or authority;
- expose character through evasion, precision, cruelty, humor, or silence;
- force a decision.

Planning dialogue works best when it includes, where appropriate:

```text
Proposal → objection → identified risk → local or occupational knowledge → pressured decision
```

Avoid procedural inventories where every participant agrees and merely lists steps.

## Exposition Disguised as Speech

Flag characters who:

- explain shared family history both already know;
- summarize the plot for the reader;
- repeat names, dates, and relationships unnaturally;
- state another person's established motive as fact;
- deliver information without a reason to speak now;
- ask questions solely so another character can explain.

### Repairs

- Give one speaker incomplete or false knowledge.
- Turn history into accusation, correction, blame, refusal, or bargaining.
- Let status or risk determine what is withheld.
- Move neutral facts into brief narration when conflict does not belong in speech.
- Cut facts already demonstrated.

## Long Speech and Theatricality

Length is not the problem. Judge:

- Does this speaker plausibly use long form?
- Does the cadence fit education, faith, occupation, region, and mood?
- Is the listener resisting, changing, or choosing?
- Does the speech alter the scene?
- Is the imagery available to this viewpoint and life experience?

Flag polished moral summaries delivered in immediate danger when the character has no reason or capacity to perform them. Flag ornamental threats any stock gunfighter could say.

## Dialect and Grammar

Rough grammar is acceptable when it is:

- intentional;
- consistent;
- comprehensible;
- speaker-specific;
- plausible for the supplied region, class, occupation, and period.

Narration remains clear by default unless the work deliberately uses a voice-heavy narrator.

Avoid exhaustive **phonetic** spelling, which can slow reading and reduce characters to accent. Prefer vocabulary, syntax, idiom, and selective markers.

Do not correct a distinctive voice into formal English merely because it is nonstandard. Correct ambiguity, accidental inconsistency, or unreadable density.

## Modern Psychological Phrasing

Flag language whose conceptual frame sounds modern and unsupported by character or narrator, especially clinical, therapeutic, corporate, bureaucratic, or internet-shaped phrasing.

Do not assume historical people lacked emotional vocabulary. Test the exact words, metaphor system, education, religion, medicine, region, and narrative register.

## Figurative Language

Prefer concrete imagery available to the viewpoint when fitting:

- work;
- weather;
- animals;
- iron;
- dust;
- rope;
- fire;
- blood;
- wood;
- stone;
- water;
- tools and machinery.

Flag:

- abstract nouns acting theatrically;
- repeated ornament that slows pressure;
- comparisons outside the character's knowledge;
- automatic weather symbolism;
- generic dust, sunset, and gun-smoke imagery that adds no specific perception;
- metaphors stacked until action loses clarity.

A lyrical narrator may use a broader image field than a close, minimally educated viewpoint. Calibrate first.

## Prose Clarity

Check:

- clear sentence subjects;
- stable **pronoun** reference;
- logical paragraph focus;
- concrete verbs;
- unnecessary filter phrases;
- duplicated interpretation;
- repeated sentence shapes;
- transitions between perception, memory, and action;
- chronology within the paragraph;
- whether the viewpoint knows what the narration claims.

Separate weak wording from weak event design. If a sound escape is confusing because “he” refers to three men, classify it as Prose. If the body positions are impossible, classify it as Story.

## Action Clarity

Track:

- starting spatial positions;
- subject continuity;
- weapon and weapon hand;
- line of sight and **line of fire**;
- cover;
- distance and terrain;
- object location;
- horse, wagon, door, window, stair, roof, and ladder positions;
- injury effects;
- movement order;
- immediate physical consequence.

The reader need not receive a diagram, but must understand enough to anticipate danger and believe the result.

## Common Character Failures

- competence without an unsolved human need;
- sidekick whose only want is helping the hero;
- antagonist defined only by menace;
- woman used as reward, witness, or hostage without her own objective;
- community treated as a grateful mass;
- mentor used only to provide advice and die;
- mysterious stranger whose mystery replaces personality;
- development summarized after the decisive choice rather than shown during it;
- every capable person making the same practical decision.

## Repair Forms

Use specific repairs:

- Cut duplicated interpretation after visible behavior.
- Replace explanation with a choice, refusal, delay, mistake, tool use, or physical consequence.
- Differentiate syntax and vocabulary by speaker rather than adding phonetic spelling.
- Break exposition with disagreement, unequal knowledge, or a reason not to answer.
- Give a supporting character a goal that conflicts with the protagonist's convenience.
- Clarify spatial sequence through concrete positions and one action per causal step.
- Preserve rough dialect while correcting ambiguous reference.
- Replace a generic threat with language tied to occupation, relationship, debt, or present leverage.
- Let a character's main skill create a liability when applied to the wrong problem.

Do not rewrite the author's passage unless a separate rewrite request is made.
