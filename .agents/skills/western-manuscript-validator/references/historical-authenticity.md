# Historical Authenticity

Historical validation must be active but bounded. Check details that affect belief, action, causality, or the story's promise. Do not turn unfamiliarity into certainty.

## Confidence Labels

Use exactly one label per finding.

- **Confirmed issue** — supplied or verified evidence directly conflicts with the detail.
- **Likely issue** — strong reason for concern exists, but exact model, locality, variant, or context may change the result.
- **Verification required** — evidence is insufficient; name exactly what must be checked.
- **Historically plausible** — the detail fits the supplied year, region, and circumstances, or is a reasonable genre simplification that does not break story logic.

“Unfamiliar” is not a confidence label. A detail may be unusual and still be plausible.


## Label Application Examples

- Use **Confirmed issue** when the source itself supplies a story year earlier than the named object's stated production date, or when an authoritative source directly contradicts the claim.
- Use **Likely issue** when a detail strongly conflicts with normal period practice but a local exception, conversion, variant, or unusual access could still exist.
- Use **Verification required** when route, model, locality, weather, or institutional facts are missing.
- Use **Historically plausible** when the supplied context fits or when a minor genre compression preserves physical and causal truth.

## Finding Format

Use:

```text
[Confidence label] Detail
Reason:
Verification needed:
Story impact: Critical / Major / Moderate / Minor
```

When no further verification is needed, write `Verification needed: None on the supplied evidence.`

## Primary Layer Guidance

Historical confidence and problem layer are separate judgments. Use **Tone** when a dated object, phrase, institution, or behavior breaks the promised era/region register. Use **Story** when historical feasibility breaks travel, action, access, evidence, medicine, or the antagonist plan. Use **Continuity** when the current detail conflicts with supplied prior material. Use **Prose** only when the fact is sound but named or explained confusingly. Use **Pacing** when procedural treatment delays or skips consequence. Choose one primary layer rather than treating “historical” as a sixth layer.

## Establish Context First

Before judging, identify:

- exact year or bounded period;
- territory, state, nation, county, or border region;
- season and elevation when travel or weather matters;
- settlement type and access to roads, stage, rail, freight, telegraph, post, military, or county government;
- subgenre tolerance;
- whether the detail is central to plot or incidental texture.

If any required condition is missing, use **Verification required** rather than inventing it.

## Firearms

Check:

- model name and production period;
- place and likelihood of availability;
- civilian, military, law-enforcement, or occupational context;
- full introduction naming followed by clear shortened naming in action;
- loading system and reload method;
- magazine or cylinder capacity;
- loading state and safe carry practice appropriate to the weapon and context;
- sights, barrel length, weight, and practical use when central;
- black-powder smoke and fouling when sustained fire matters;
- recoil and injury limits;
- which hand operates, braces, reloads, or supports the weapon;
- consistency across scenes.

Do not hard-code an encyclopedic firearm catalog into the skill. For disputed models, dates, factory variants, conversions, and regional availability, use authoritative verification.

### Naming discipline

At introduction, a full or useful name may establish the object. During fast action, shorten naturally when clarity remains:

- full introduction: manufacturer/model/caliber where relevant;
- action: “the Colt,” “the Winchester,” “the rifle,” or another unambiguous short form.

Do not insist on model names when the viewpoint would not know them.

## Ammunition

Check:

- cartridge or cap-and-ball compatibility;
- caliber consistency;
- whether rifle and revolver share ammunition only when technically plausible;
- carried quantity;
- reload speed and available cover;
- loose powder, paper cartridge, metallic cartridge, primer, cap, or shell requirements;
- shotgun gauge and shell type;
- ammunition access after travel, rain, loss, or capture;
- whether every fired round has an accountable source in a long action sequence.

A central ammunition contradiction can be a Story issue as well as a historical flag.

## Transport and Travel

Travel-time claims require assumptions. Check:

- distance;
- road versus cross-country route;
- elevation and terrain;
- season and weather;
- water and forage;
- horse condition;
- remount availability;
- wagon type, load, wheel condition, and team;
- river crossings and ferry access;
- rest and sleep;
- rider skill and urgency;
- stage or freight schedule;
- railroad reach;
- delays caused by injury, livestock, snow, mud, heat, or darkness.

Do not validate “two days” or “thirty-six hours” without a route model. State the variables required.

## Communication

Check:

- whether telegraph service reaches the place and date;
- where the nearest station is;
- stage, mail, freight, military, railroad, courier, or private rider routes;
- message composition and delivery chain;
- whether offices are open and staffed;
- whether weather or distance interrupts service;
- whether sender and recipient are reachable;
- whether the message can physically arrive before the event it changes.

A telegraph existing somewhere in the territory does not mean every settlement has access.

## Law and Institutions

Check:

- territory versus state status;
- town, county, territorial, federal, tribal, or military jurisdiction;
- sheriff, deputy, town marshal, U.S. marshal, constable, judge, governor, and military roles;
- appointment versus election where central;
- warrants, custody, transfer, posse authority, and prisoner handling;
- county seat location;
- newspaper, post, land office, court, and records access when relevant;
- the difference between what was legal, what was common, and what isolated communities could enforce.

Judge story function separately from exact procedure. A minor omitted form may be acceptable; jurisdiction that makes the climax impossible is not.

## Medicine, Recovery, and Injury

Check:

- wound location, depth, bleeding, and contamination;
- bone, tendon, nerve, lung, abdominal, and vascular consequences;
- infection, fever, shock, dehydration, and blood loss;
- available doctor, surgeon, midwife, nurse, herbal knowledge, or improvised care;
- laudanum, alcohol, antisepsis, bandaging, splinting, extraction, and surgery limits;
- mobility and sleep;
- repeated mounting, lifting, twisting, climbing, grappling, and recoil;
- recovery time;
- scarring, numbness, grip failure, weakness, reduced endurance, and weather sensitivity;
- whether later action respects the injury.

Do not equate survival with full function. A wound may close while strength, sensation, breathing, or endurance remains impaired.

## Occupations and Work Procedure

Check only the work the story actually uses.

### Cattle and ranch work

- trail hierarchy and positions;
- herd size and pace;
- watches, bedground, water, remuda, rope work, branding, doctoring, and supply;
- how leaving position affects others;
- animal behavior and fatigue.

### Horses and tack

- saddle, cinch, latigo, bridle, bit, reins, hobbles, picket, shoes, hoof condition, and remount rotation;
- realistic response to noise, blood, fire, weather, and inexperienced handling.

### Freighting and stage work

- wagon load, team, brake, wheel, axle, harness, relay, station, schedule, guard, strongbox, and road condition.

### Other work

When present, verify mining, printing, telegraphy, military duty, farming, medicine, blacksmithing, law, hotel work, saloon work, teaching, domestic labor, and trade through region- and period-specific sources.

Technical detail should generate decisions or belief. Decorative procedure does not earn accuracy by volume.

## Material Culture

Check central details involving:

- clothing and footwear;
- food, coffee, preservation, cookware, and supply;
- lamps, candles, stoves, matches, and fuel;
- tools, rope, wire, nails, leather, canvas, and repair;
- buildings, glass, locks, furniture, printing equipment, and paper;
- money, wages, prices, credit, and banking when plot-critical;
- photography, newspapers, books, letters, and record keeping;
- common versus luxury objects;
- how far an object must travel to reach the region.

Require a specific year and region before declaring a practice wrong.

## Weather, Terrain, and Environment

Check:

- season and elevation;
- daylight length;
- snow, rain, hail, wind, heat, cold, mud, drought, and flood;
- river level and crossing conditions;
- vegetation and ground cover;
- mountain, basin, prairie, desert, canyon, forest, and borderland conditions;
- animal and human response;
- whether weather changes route, pace, visibility, fire, tracks, or evidence.

Weather should not appear only when symbolism or action convenience requires it.

## Vocabulary and Idiom

Check:

- known anachronisms;
- occupational terms;
- legal and institutional terms;
- technology words;
- regional vocabulary;
- profanity and euphemism;
- narrator register versus spoken vocabulary;
- whether a phrase is presented as common idiom or character-specific invention.

A novel may use clear modern-readable narration without giving characters distinctly modern concepts. Do not reject a phrase merely because it is unfamiliar.

## Research Hierarchy

When outside verification is allowed, prefer:

1. Primary documents, manufacturer records, period newspapers, government records, diaries, letters, manuals, catalogs, maps, and timetables.
2. Museums, historical societies, academic histories, and official archives.
3. Specialist references with transparent sourcing.
4. General summaries for orientation only, not decisive contradiction.

When reliable verification is unavailable, use **Verification required** rather than guessing. If sources disagree, state the disagreement and what exact variant, locality, or date controls the answer.

## Story Impact

Historical precision matters most when the detail controls:

- the central weapon;
- a travel deadline;
- a message arriving in time;
- jurisdiction or custody;
- an injury and later capability;
- evidence survival or interpretation;
- work procedure that causes the danger;
- weather or terrain that determines the route;
- a character's claimed expertise.

Minor texture errors may be Minor. A central impossibility may be Major or Critical.

## Subgenre Tolerance

A minor genre simplification may be **Historically plausible** in action/pulp work when it does not break:

- physical possibility;
- causality;
- stakes;
- custody;
- reader orientation;
- a central historical promise.

Compression is not permission to make central weapons, travel, institutions, injuries, or work systems impossible. Classic/epic and revisionist work often promises more accumulation and precision, but no subgenre is automatically accurate.

## Common Errors in Historical Review

- treating a current internet summary as stronger than a primary source;
- declaring a weapon impossible without checking conversion or variant;
- confirming travel without route and remount assumptions;
- confusing a town marshal with a county sheriff;
- curing nerve damage because a wound closed;
- assuming every settlement has telegraph access;
- treating every unusual phrase as anachronistic;
- forcing procedural detail that has no story function;
- scoring uncertainty as proven error.

Use the labels to preserve confidence and honesty.
