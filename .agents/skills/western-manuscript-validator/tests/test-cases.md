# Western Manuscript Validator — Behavioral Test Cases

These cases test instruction behavior. Each case supplies only the user-style input. Expected classifications, required findings, forbidden responses, and pass criteria live in `expected-behaviors.md`.

Use the validator as written. Do not import project-specific rules or invent missing context.

## Primary Problem Layer

### LAYER-STORY — Identification without evidence

The robber wore a flour sack over his head from the moment he entered the depot until he rode away. He never spoke. He used a common bay horse with no visible brand and carried an ordinary revolver. The depot clerk remained under the ticket counter and saw only the robber's boots and coat hem. No one followed the tracks because rain erased them within minutes.

At the sheriff's office, the clerk studies six local men through the window. He points to Silas Dent and says, "That's him. I'd know those eyes anywhere." The sheriff accepts the identification and arrests Silas. The chapter later treats the clerk's certainty as reliable evidence. No distinctive boots, coat, gait, height, injury, scent, weapon, horse sign, prior knowledge, or accidental glimpse is supplied. Silas's arrest launches the rest of the plot, and every other character acts as though the clerk could genuinely identify him.

### LAYER-PROSE — Logical escape, unclear writing

Caleb kicked the door shut behind Rowan as he crossed the loft. He saw him reach for the rope beside the window, but it had already been cut. The guard fired from below. He ducked behind the grain sacks while he pulled the ladder away, which made him fall against the railing. Rowan threw it through the opening, and Caleb caught his arm before he went over. He struck him with the pistol, then he let go and landed on the wagon canvas. The guard ran outside, but he was already beneath the axle. He crawled toward the alley while Rowan climbed after him. Caleb fired once at the lamp, and when it broke he crossed the yard before the guard could see which man had taken the horse.

The underlying escape is meant to be straightforward: Rowan distracts the guard, Caleb drops onto a wagon, hides beneath it, crosses the dark yard, and takes a horse. The paragraph's pronouns and positions are the only source of confusion.

### LAYER-TONE — Modern therapeutic register

Trail boss Arlen Vey has just discovered that one of his riders concealed a fever and nearly fell beneath the herd. The immediate facts and the conflict are coherent. Arlen tells him:

"I need you to honor your body's boundaries and communicate your needs before your nervous system becomes overwhelmed. This crew has to create a safe space where you can be vulnerable without feeling judged. I hear that your independence matters, but your pattern of self-abandonment is affecting the group's emotional regulation. Going forward, I need you to practice transparency and let us support your healing journey."

The rider answers in the same register, saying he "didn't have the emotional bandwidth" to disclose the fever. The story is set in Texas in 1884. Neither man is written elsewhere as unusually educated, medical, religious, or eccentric in speech.

### LAYER-CONTINUITY — Disabled hand forgotten

Prior-book note supplied by the author: During the Book 1 climax, Mara Holt's left hand was crushed beneath a wagon wheel. The doctor saved the hand, but the last two fingers remained rigid, grip strength never fully returned, and cold weather caused numbness. The epilogue established that she could fire a revolver right-handed but could not safely hold a long gun without support.

Book 2 excerpt, six weeks later in freezing weather: Mara runs uphill carrying a full-length buffalo rifle in both hands. She drops to one knee, shoulders it without a rest, works the action rapidly, and fires four carefully aimed shots. She then hangs from a roof edge by her left hand while pulling another person up with her right. No recovery, brace, pain, altered grip, assistance, or explanation appears. The narration does not present the feat as surprising or damaging.

### LAYER-PACING — Skip across consequence

The chapter ends with twelve-year-old Eli finding his mother dead beside the winter well. His older sisters arrive, the youngest begins screaming, and their father is still three days away with the cattle. Eli sees a bloody strip of cloth caught on the pump handle and recognizes the stitching from a neighbor's coat.

The next chapter begins: "Three months later, spring softened the north field." The mother has already been buried. The father is home. The neighbor has left the county. The family now distrusts one another, but the burial, the father's return, Eli's accusation, the sisters' reactions, the handling of the cloth, and the decision not to pursue the neighbor are summarized in two sentences. The story resumes with Eli repairing fence and thinking about revenge. None of the immediate aftermath appears as a scene.

## Causality and Antagonist Logic

### CAUSE-FRAME-PLAUSIBLE — Frame-up with access and witness path

A saloon owner wants Deputy Vale blamed for a murder. Earlier, the owner pays a stable boy to copy Vale's boot heel after Vale leaves the boots for repair. During a crowded dance, the owner steals Vale's distinctive brass tobacco tin. He kills the victim in an alley while rain softens the mud, presses the copied heel beside the body, and drops the tin near the victim's hand. A laundress sees a man in Vale's coat enter the alley but cannot see his face. The owner arranged for Vale's coat to be borrowed for "cleaning" that afternoon. At dawn, the laundress reports what she saw, the sheriff finds the print and tin, and the town draws the wrong conclusion. The owner risks exposure if the stable boy, laundress, or coat owner compares times.

### CAUSE-FRAME-SELFEXPOSE — Planting the villain's own signature

Cattle broker Harrow murders his partner and wants a passing drifter blamed. Harrow leaves his own custom silver spur beside the body. Every rancher in the county recognizes the spur because Harrow commissioned the pair and wears them at public auctions. He then tells the sheriff that the drifter must have stolen it, though no theft was reported and Harrow is still wearing the matching spur. The story calls this a brilliant frame-up and gives the sheriff no doubts.

### CAUSE-BETRAYAL-NOACCESS — Secret route leaked without access

Only the marshal and the doctor know that the witness will leave through the abandoned quarry at midnight. They decide this in a locked room and write nothing down. The marshal then privately changes the departure to eleven-thirty and tells only the witness. At eleven-twenty, ambushers are already waiting at the exact quarry exit. The story later reveals that the blacksmith betrayed them, but the blacksmith never entered the room, spoke to any participant, handled their horses, saw the route, or had a stated source inside the jail.

### CAUSE-COINCIDENCE-SOLUTION — Missing witness arrives at climax

For ten chapters, both sides search for the only surviving witness to a stage robbery. No route, destination, message, relative, habit, or likely hiding place is established. During the final public accusation in a saloon, the missing witness happens to walk through the front door, recognizes the villain, and provides the exact testimony needed to settle every disputed fact. The witness says he stopped only because he saw lamplight while passing through town.

### CAUSE-SABOTAGE-HELPS — Sabotage clarifies the case

A saboteur wants a wagon expedition to fail. First he cuts a harness, but the clean cut proves the damage was deliberate and narrows access to three men. Next he poisons a water barrel with a rare bitter herb, which directly identifies the only herbalist in the crew. Then he releases the remounts but leaves his personal rope tied to the gate. Each act is discovered before causing delay, injury, lost supplies, or route change. The expedition continues on schedule while the suspect list becomes steadily clearer. The saboteur never changes method or objective.
